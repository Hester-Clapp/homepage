What is CSET?

  • Cambridge is expected to grow significantly in the coming decades
  • New homes need to be built in the surrounding area
  • A new transport project is planned to connect these new communities to the Cambridge Biomedical Campus

The Greater Cambridge Partnership is proposing a bus-only road to connect the CBC with the villages of Shelford, Stapleford, Sawston and Babraham, as well as the Babraham Research Institute and Granta Park. The route will end at a park-and-ride site near the A11 and A1307 for onward travel to Linton and Haverhill.

Proposed route of the CSET busway. Source: Greater Cambridge Partnership

Technology used by the CSET

Unlike the Cambridgeshire Guided Busway to St Ives, the CSET will not make use of physical guidance technology. Instead, buses will be optically guided by markings in the road.

Optical guided buses (trackless trams) in 宜Yí宾bīn, China. Source: Wikimedia

Benefits of optical guidance

  • Reduced infrastructure cost
  • Route can be used by service and emergency vehicles
  • Higher precision than normal buses
  • Reduced need for driver intervention

Drawbacks of optical guidance

  • More expensive onboard technology
  • More road maintenance required
  • Poor reliability in snow, rain and fog
  • Cannot handle unexpected circumstances such as roadworks

but the real reason optical guidance is being used is:

  • The GCP is using the Transport and Works Act 1992, which is designed for (re)building railways and tram systems. If the system were to just use a normal road, the scheme would not be eligible for government approval and funding of land aquisition, construction and operation.

Legal Issue - Legal loopholes may encourage unnecessary use of technology. This costs more money to the taxpayer for little benefit.

Cambridgeshire Autonomous Metro

A large portion of the CSET plan is carried over from a scheme proposed by the GCP in 2017 and subsequently cancelled. This scheme (called the Cambridgeshire Autonomous Metro or CAM) was to be much more ambitious, featuring level 4 autonomy which gives buses the ability to operate without human intervention. Furthermore, these buses would run further into the city, serving the rail station and city centre.

Dangers of autonomous vehicles

Elaine Herzberg was the first person to be killed by a driverless vehicle, in March 2018.1 She walking across a road in Tempe Arizona where there was no crosswalk, and pushing a bicycle by her side.1 There is no established walkway from the nearest crosswalk to the brick median where Elaine was crossing,1 and the route was overgrown with bushes and cacti.

The site of the collision on North Mill Avenue, Tempe Arizona. Source: NTSB Crash Report

Elaine was detected by the car's sensors 5.6 seconds before the collision, but the software struggled to make sense of the data it was receiving and failed to evaluate what she was and whether she was in the path of the car. She was classified at various points as a vehicle, a bicycle, and "other", and only at 1.2 seconds to impact was she correctly identified as crossing the vehicle's path.1 The car's passenger was not paying attention and only took control of steering just 0.02 seconds before the vehicle collided with Elaine at 39mph.1

Social Issue - While driverless cars may be better at detecting hazards than human drivers, they may not take correct action to avoid them.

Algorithmic Bias

Traffic collisions similar to Elaine's example may be amplified by algorithmic bias. For example, AI systems have previously failed to identify darker skin colours due to their absence in the training data used. What would it mean for society if our traffic classifies members of BAME communities as "other"?

Furthermore, driverless cars are largely being tested in the United States of America for a variety of reasons, but this could have unexpected impacts on their adoption elsewhere. Urban planning and street layout vary all across the world, and American streets are designed to prioritise cars and road traffic. If driverless cars are "growing up" in a place where the car is king, what are they going to do when they have to share the road with other pedestrians and cyclists? Furthermore, driverless cars are unlikely to be tested extensively in less developed countries, where driving is more difficult and profits are lower.

Ethical Issue - Algorithms are as biased as the data they are given, and these biases can be very difficult to change.

Cyber Attacks on autonomous vehicles

In June 2019, experts from Regulus Cyber managed to hack the GPS navigation system of a Tesla Model 3 to demonstrate the vulnerability of driverless car technologies.2 Using a method known as spoofing where false GPS signals were sent to the car, they were able to force the car to head to a different destination than the one chosen by the driver. 2 They were also able to track the driver's location, compromising their privacy and safety.

This has severe implications for a driverless bus. A hacker could potentially re-route the bus or purposefully cause road nuissance with such a large vehicle. This could seriously disrupt significant parts of the transport system - if a bus was hacked on the CSET, it could block the whole busway for all other vehicles

What does this mean for Cambridge?

As Cambridge looks to the future of its transportation infrastructure, the introduction of autonomous buses and cars could present serious safety concerns. In a city known for its narrow streets, a high volume of cyclists and unpredictable pedestrian traffic, it provides a challenging environment for autonomous vehicles to be tested in. But Cambridge wouldn't be the first city in the world, or even the UK to trial autonomous buses.

In Edinburgh, the CAVForth scheme was launched in May 2023, connecting Edinburgh and Fife with level 4 autonomous buses.3 However, this route runs primarily along major roads, and has at several points required manual intervention.4 Furthermore, the service was discontinued in February 2025 due to low ridership,4 perhaps suggesting that our cities and their residents are not ready for driverless buses.

One of the autonomous buses used on the CAVForth service

While it could be seen as preparation for the future, this does raise the question of whether Cambridge needs driverless buses after all, if they are not currently the best option. While AV investment has largely come from the private sector up until now, public transport projects like CSET are funded mainly by the government and local councils, using money raised from residents' taxes. Their purpose is not to make the most profit, but provide the best service to the majority of residents at the lowest cost, and proprietary technology does not offer this level of certainty and quality.

Social Issue - Funding for emerging technology from the public sector is at the detriment of taxpayers

A better solution?

This section is about my opinion on the CSET specifically; you do not have to agree. No one who knows me will be surprised to hear that I like trains. But in this case, I passionately believe that high-tech bus roads are simply an inferior solution compared to simply rebuilding the lost railway to Haverhill. While doing this would cost more money, the benefits it would provide are far more long-term and sustainable than the CSET busway.

Firstly, trains have a higher passenger capacity than buses, and can easily be scaled to carry more people. After the construction of Northstowe, the Cambridgeshire Guided Busway is already struggling with capacity-related issues,5 and there is little that can be done to aleviate these. If the plan truly is to develop the commuter belt along the A1307 corridor, a busway is simply not going to be able to cope.

Secondly trains are simply faster. The predicted scheme predicts a journey time from Sawston to Cambridge of 30 minutes,6 while the train from Pampisford in 1948 took just 18.7 The Cambridgeshire Guided Busway has also failed to decrease journey times, with Cambridge to Huntingdon taking 85 minutes in spite of the busway. 8

Thirdly, railways are heavily standardised, and a new railway will be compatible with existing tracks. If a railway is built, trains can access it from the existing tracks so no work needs to be done in the most expensive part of Cambridge. If the busway was built and then an extension to the city centre was needed, the optical guideway would need to be built and maintained all the way into Cambridge, regularly costing the council a lot of money.

Leading on from the previous point, the proposed bus stops are not conveniently located for the villages they serve, often being located on the very edge. While it may spur development, the existing station at Shelford is conveniently located within walking distance of Great Shelford, Little Shelford and Stapleford, and millions have already been spent on a new Cambridge South station at the biomedical campus.

Fifthly, transport by rail is much more energy efficient and sustainable. In the context of the climate crisis, we must consider the CO2 emissions of our transportation choices, and trains are by far the best option on this front. Even electric buses lose significant amounts of energy from tyre deformation.
