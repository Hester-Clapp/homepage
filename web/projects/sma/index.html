Shape-Memory Alloys

What are shape-memory alloys?

A shape-memory alloy is a type of smart material that reacts to heat and changes its shape. It can be plastically deformedDeformed in such a way that the material stays deformed, but it will "remember" its original shape, which will be restored if the material is heated above a certain temperature.[1]

The most common type of SMA is a Nickel-Titanium alloy known as NiNickelTiTitaniumNOLNaval Ordanance Laboratory. It was first discovered at the Naval Ordanance Laboratory in 1959 by William J Buehler and Frederick E Wang.[2]


When fabricating NiTiNOL, the ratio of Nickel to Titanium has a drastic effect on the transformation temperature. Just a 1% increase in Nickel concentration equates to a change of ~100°C![3] The alloy is worked at temperatures of around 800°C and formed into the right shape, after which point it is annealed at ~350°C to determine the original form. The transformation temperatureThe temperature at which the SMA changes structure can also be adjusted during this time.[3]

How do shape-memory alloys work?

Explanation:

The shape-memory effect is caused by the material alternating between two different structures.

Below the transformation temperatureThe temperature at which the SMA changes structure, the structure is known as martensiteA type of crystal structure which resembles a normal metal, which in the simulation has rhombus-shaped crystals. This is malleable, and can be plastically deformedDeformed in such a way that the material stays deformed with only a little stress.

When the bar is heated above the transformation temperatureThe temperature at which the SMA changes structure, the structure changes to a structure called austeniteA type of crystal structure which behaves like a spring, which has square crystals in the simulation. This makes the material super-elasticWhen the material is bent, it returns to its original shape by changing its crystal structure since this structure cannot be plastically deformedDeformed in such a way that the material stays deformed, causing the bar to spring back into the shape it was originally annealed in.

When the bar cools back down, the structure returns to martensiteA type of crystal structure which resembles a normal metal, but the overall shape doesn't change.

Properties

Density: 6500 kg m-3 [4]
Ultimate Tensile Stress: 8.6 GPa [4]
Young's Modulus: ~30GPa (martensiteA type of crystal structure which resembles a normal metal) [4] ~80GPa (austeniteA type of crystal structure which behaves like a spring) [4]

Martensitic NiTiNOL has a very low yield stress, and can be plastically deformedDeformed in such a way that the material stays deformed with a small amount of force.[1] This is caused by the crystals changing which direction they point in, as demonstrated by the simulation.[1]

This deformation is recoverable through heating, but it does not allow the material to change volume.

Of course, with shape-memory alloys, stress is only half of the story. The material lengthens and contracts when it changes between the martensiteA type of crystal structure which resembles a normal metal and austeniteA type of crystal structure which behaves like a spring structures, in addition to normal heat expansion.

NiTiNOL behaves differently to other metals due to its super-elasticityWhen the material is bent, it returns to its original shape by changing its crystal structure. The loading and unloading curves form a hysteresis loop, allowing the material to elastically deform, similar to rubber and other natural materials such as hair or bone.[5]

Medical Applications

NiTiNOL is well-suited to use inside the body, due to its elasticity as well as its resistance to kinks and corrosion.[5]

The shape-memory effect means it is possible to make self-expanding stents. They can be collapsed, and inserted into blood vessels through tiny holes. Then they are heated to expand and deploy to the correct size.[5]

Industrial Applications

SMAs have also been used to make actuatorsA mechanical component which can be controlled to change length or apply force, utilising the ability to change length when heated by an electrical current.[6] Similarly, they can be used in temperature sensors, by deforming at the specific transformation temperatureThe temperature at which the SMA changes structure and completing a circuit.[6]

Mechanisms involving shape-memory alloys tend to be simpler, with fewer moving parts, and also lighter than conventional mechanisms. However, they lack the same longevity and reliability after heavy use, as the material degrades as it is repeatedly heated, cooled and deformed.[6] As it wears out, it may become less flexible, or fail to return to its precise original shape, making it less desirable.[6]

Conclusion

Shape-memory alloys are an interesting and unique family of materials. They are unlike other metals with their ability to be plastically deformedDeformed in such a way that the material stays deformed, or become elastic when heated.

This unique behaviour makes them suitable for a variety of niche applications. However, they are difficult to manufacture and prone to degradation, and other materials are usually favourable due to cost.

65 years since their discovery, shape-memory alloys have not yet been widely adopted, and likely to remain a quirky yet impractical material in the future.
